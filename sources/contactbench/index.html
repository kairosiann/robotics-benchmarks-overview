Motivation and Overview

What constitutes success in contact-rich manipulation?

While many prior works focus on insertion and peg-in-hole tasks or sustained-force tasks such as wiping and peeling, everyday object use spans a much broader range of contact behaviors. ContactBench 1.0 offers a concrete, example-driven answer: we identify eight contact-rich skills drawn from routine human activity, instantiated through 36 reference tasks, more than 40 3D-printable assets, and their simulation counterparts. The assets are designed to produce realistic contact force profiles, be reproducible across popular 3D printers, and support controlled variations to evaluate a robot system's ability to adapt to novel task conditions that might or might not be obvious to the vision modality alone. Together, the benchmark gives the community common ground for comparing methods. Our benchmarking experiments show contact remains hard for current policies, with or without tactile sensing.

Policy Benchmarking Experiments

We collect data with a customized UMI hand-held gripper and evaluate two vision-only policy learning methods, Diffusion Policy (DP) and π0.5, and their representative visual-tactile variants, Reactive Diffusion Policy (RDP) and Foundation Tactile Policy (FTP-1) on six tasks from our benchmark:

What are challenging for vision-only policies on the selected tasks?

We find vision-only policies struggle to perceive and react to contact events:

  1. 1 Establishing and maintaining contact The policies often miss or lose contact between the fingers, the object, and the environment.
  2. 2 Precise alignment The policies don't make the small, visually subtle corrections needed to align an in-hand object with its target.
  3. 3 Avoiding unsafe forceful interactions The policies often collide hard with task objects and the environment, which can fail the task or be unsafe.
  4. 4 Recognizing task-critical states The policies can't tell some task-critical states apart by sight, such as how many chopsticks are grasped.

Not specific to contact‑rich manipulation

  1. 5 Lack of memory The policies repeat finished stages that still look unfinished, like tightening an already-tight screw.

Do visual-tactile policies address the challenges above?

Although intuitively, effective integration of force and/or tactile sensing can address the challenges above, we did not find RDP and FTP-1 to meaningfully improve on their vision-only base policies in success rate, task progress, interaction force, or completion time (as shown in the quantitative results earlier). Across their rollouts, we observed three aspects that limited the benefit of the added tactile input:

  1. 1Sensor coverage limited the tactile information available to the policy

    Because GelSight Mini only captures contact inside the gripper fingertip, it cannot perceive and avoid collisions happening to other parts of the gripper finger.

    Although vision-only policies performed poorly on retrieve single utensil from bin and pick up card from platform, we could not collect demonstrations or train visual-tactile policies for them because GelSight Mini's fingertip-only coverage and bulky form factor made it unsuitable for either task.

  2. 2Tactile-guided behaviors weren't learned

    Despite the availability of tactile feedback, we find visual-tactile policies to fail in the same ways as the vision-only policies.

  3. 3Policies acted on incidental tactile signals rather than task-relevant ones

    In tighten screw with Allen key, policies frequently terminate the tightening motion after the Allen key slips in hand, even though in-hand slip can occur naturally as the task progresses and does not reliably indicate that the screw has been fully tightened.

How well do policies generalize to task changes that are hard to perceive visually?

We conduct two behavioral generalization case studies on turn doorknob to open door, a task where all four policies achieve near-perfect success rates. We alter the task conditions in two ways:

Behavioral Variation: Required Rotation Amount to Unlock Door

While the policies were trained on a door that requires 60° rotation to unlock, we test them on two additional doors that require 72° and 80° rotation to unlock. The three doors are visually indistinguishable.

We find that RDP retains significantly higher success rates on the 72° and 80° variants compared to the other three policies. After investigating how much each policy actually rotates the door under the three task conditions, we find that RDP is the only policy that demonstrated adaptation to the changing task demand:

This shows that tactile-augmented policies have the potential to achieve better behavioral generalization even when they offer no gain for in-distribution task settings. However, the extent to which tactile sensing helps also depends on how tactile signals are integrated into the policy.

Visual + Behavioral Variation: Smaller Doorknob Diameter

While the policies were trained on a door with a 60 mm diameter doorknob, we test them on a door with a smaller 55 mm diameter doorknob. Although this change can be perceived visually, the visual differences are subtle.

Across 10 rollouts per policy (we did not run the full 30-rollout protocol since the failure was immediate and consistent across every rollout of every policy), all four policies achieved 0% success rate, and they were nowhere near establishing a stable grasp and completing the task:

We further investigate the gripping width each policy reached when attempting to grasp the doorknob. None of the policies attempted to close the gripper more for the smaller doorknob, indicating that they failed to learn a key aspect of grasping: adapting to the object’s size to achieve stable contact.
