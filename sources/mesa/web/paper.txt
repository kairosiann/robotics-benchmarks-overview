Abstract
Despite growing interest in foundation models for robotic manipulation, systematic evaluation of their capabilities remains elusive. Existing benchmarks are either narrow and saturated, offering little signal on SOTA methods, broadly scoped, providing diversity at the cost of controlled experimentation, or require training on large, messy open-source datasets. We introduce MESA, an evaluation framework designed for precise, reproducible measurement of language-conditioned policy generalization. MESA provides five evaluation suites that isolate in-distribution performance and four distinct axes of generalization: spatial configuration, object instance, object category, and subtask composition, enabling researchers to diagnose where and why policies fail, not just whether they fail.
To populate these suites at scale, we develop MESA-Gen, a pipeline for task and demonstration generation built by improving MimicGen.
We instantiate the framework on two platforms and release both: the single-arm MESA suite, and BiMESA, the first large-scale benchmark for bimanual generalization.
We evaluate seven baseline methods spanning diffusion policies, VLM-based approaches, and pretrained VLAs, finding that the strongest model achieves only 52% on MESA and 49% on BiMESA, providing meaningful signal while leaving ample room for progress.
MESA rankings are broadly consistent with RoboArena scores, suggesting that MESA captures method-level differences relevant beyond simulation.

Benchmarks shape the trajectory of research.
They define what the community measures, and hence what it optimizes for.

Simulation offers a path toward reproducible evaluation, but existing benchmarks suffer from several complementary limitations.

We propose that evaluation sets should isolate

where

why

fast iteration

To meet these objectives, we introduce MESA, an extensible evaluation framework for language-conditioned robotic manipulation.
Our contributions are as follows:
1. MESA-Bench
, a benchmark containing single-arm Franka and bimanual YAM embodiments, each instantiated with a canonical training set and five evaluation suites targeting in-distribution performance, spatial generalization, instance generalization, category generalization, and compositional generalization.
We introduce a language-following metric based on distractor-task completion to disentangle instruction-grounding failures from control-execution failures.
2. MESA-Gen
, a framework for scalable task and dataset generation.
Researchers can curate targeted evaluations for emerging questions and synthesize the corresponding data to power their experiments.
We introduce improvements to MimicGen
[
27

that enable higher-quality data generation under greater spatial variation and support generation for novel tasks.
3. Rigorous experiments
evaluating seven methods spanning diffusion policies, VLM-based approaches, and pretrained VLAs.
In the bimanual setting, our strongest baseline achieves only 48.7% success rate, dropping to 18.6% on novel object categories, suggesting substantial headroom for future improvement.
Results correlate with real-world rankings, providing preliminary evidence that MESA captures method-level differences relevant beyond simulation.

Simulated Benchmarks.
A useful benchmark for modern RFMs should provide axis-aligned splits that isolate specific generalization axes, remain tractable to train and evaluate on academic compute, and target a setting compatible with RFM pretraining distributions.
No existing simulated benchmark satisfies all three.

On the other hand, broad suites such as RoboCasa

and Behavior-1K
[

target large mobile-manipulation or long-horizon household activities that fall outside typical RFM pretraining regimes and lack targeted OOD evaluation sets.

[

MESA satisfies all these requirements by providing axis-aligned evaluation suites in a tabletop manipulation setting, with a training set that is tractable to train on academic compute while still providing meaningful signal for modern RFMs.
Additionally, MESA is, to our knowledge, the first benchmark to test language steerability and axis-aligned generalization splits for both single-arm and bimanual embodiments.
Real-to-Sim Benchmarks.
Many recent works evaluate policies trained from large-scale real datasets in simulation
[

,

,

This paradigm has several drawbacks.
First, DROID’s scale and opaque coverage make it expensive to train on and difficult to construct OOD settings that are guaranteed to be absent from training.
Second, the dataset is messy, with many inaccurate language instructions or long meaningless actions, such that simply filtering it can yield outsized performance gains
[

On the other hand, MESA provides small, canonical datasets with sufficient diversity to benchmark language grounding and generalization without excessive scale slowing iteration.
3
Generating Benchmarks with MESA-Gen
MESA-Gen is a framework for procedurally generating manipulation tasks and corresponding demonstration data (
Fig. 2
), which we use to instantiate MESA-Bench (
§ 4
).
A core principle is that every demonstration and evaluation trajectory uses a unique scene with a unique set of distractor objects, making language following critical even in-distribution: the policy must disambiguate between multiple plausible goals.
Concretely, we specify each task as in- and out-of-distribution BDDL files
[
43
,
44
]
and use MimicGen
[
27
]
to generate in-distribution demonstrations.
Figure 2:
MESA-Gen experiment building framework
. (1) If a suitable task skeleton does not already exist, define it in Python. (2) Instantiate a task from the skeleton and generate BDDL files for the training and evaluation sets. (3) Optionally, collect a small number of source demonstrations. (4) Generate a large dataset for imitation learning. Using subtask stitching enables data generation for tasks without human data.
3.1
Generating Diverse Manipulation Tasks
The first step in MESA-Gen is to procedurally generate task variants for training and evaluation from a
task skeleton
: a Python class specifying initialization, demonstration, and goal predicates (App.
C.5
).
Given a skeleton and a set of object categories, MESA emits BDDL files for the training and evaluation sets, each with a unique scene layout, distractor set, and, optionally, varied textures, camera pose, and robot embodiment (
Fig. 2
).
Ensuring Visual Confusion:
To benchmark language steerability, we must prevent the policy from inferring the task solely from visual observations.
If a scene contains only one interactable object, the policy may ignore the language instruction and simply act on that object, a form of
causal confusion
[
45
]
.
To ensure high observation-conditioned task distribution entropy, we sample distractors such that every scene contains multiple seen object-destination pairs, as elaborated in App.
C.4
.
3.2
Scalable Data Generation with MimicGen
To generate demonstrations at scale, MESA-Gen builds on MimicGen
[
27
]
, an object-centric data generation pipeline. We extend it along two axes.
First, we introduce
subtask stitching
: rather than requiring a human demonstration for every new task, MESA-Gen composes object-centric subtasks (e.g.,
Grasp(apple)
,
On(
⋅
\cdot
, plate)
) drawn from a shared library indexed by predicate type and object semantics, enabling data generation for tasks with no human source data (App.
D.3
).
Second, we introduce
trajectory optimizations
that improve data generation quality under large source-to-target object pose differences, avoiding the unnatural sweeping motions and self-collisions that vanilla MimicGen produces under large spatial variation (App.
D.4
).
Together, these enable MESA-Gen to generate high-quality data for novel tasks under substantially greater spatial variation than prior MimicGen-based pipelines.
3.3
Evaluating Language Following
Recent VLA benchmarks have emphasized
language following
as a key capability
[
4
,
8
,
46
,
6
]
, but the term is often defined imprecisely.
To address this, MESA-Gen uses the BDDL predicate system to explicitly measure whether a policy completed the intended task or an alternative distractor task.
For each scene, we enumerate feasible distractor tasks from pairs of distractor objects and detect when a failed rollout completes one. We define the
language following rate
as
L
=
S
S
+
D
L=\frac{S}{S+D}
where
S
S
is the success rate and
D
D
is the distractor task completion rate. Intuitively, this measures the probability that, conditioned on completing some feasible task, the policy completed the correct one.
4
MESA-Bench
We use MESA-Gen to instantiate MESA-Bench, a fixed benchmark to support accessible and reproducible policy evaluation.
MESA-Bench includes two embodiments: a DROID-like single-arm Franka Panda
[
26
]
and a bimanual YAM setup. For each embodiment
we provide a training set (
§ 4.1
)
and five evaluation suites (
§ 4.2
) probing in-distribution performance and OOD performance along four axes of generalization.
Benchmark construction and per-suite details are in App.
E
.
4.1
Training Set
We provide one shared training set per embodiment, used for all evaluation sets. The single-arm set, MESA-70, contains 7,000 demonstrations across 70 tasks, while the bimanual set, BiMESA-57, contains 6,833 demonstrations across 57 tasks. We additionally provide 736 auxiliary single-arm tasks, MESA-Aux, for studying diversity scaling (
§ 5.4
). Further dataset details are provided in App.
E.4
.
4.2
Evaluation Suites
For all trials, we sample an unseen set of distractor objects and poses and generate a fixed set of initial states for unbiased comparisons across policies.
The full task lists and partition definitions are in App.
E
, and per-task success rates are in App.
B.8
.
MESA-ID
tests policy performance on in-distribution tasks: 70 single-arm tasks (MESA-70) and 57 bimanual tasks (BiMESA-57).
Each scene has an unseen set of distractor objects, meaning that policies must precisely follow language instructions to succeed in this setting.
MESA-Spatial
evaluates policies under changes in object location.
The single-arm setting contains 10 tasks: we partition the table into two halves
ℛ
ID
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ID}}
and
ℛ
OOD
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{OOD}}
(one left, one right) and only initialize the target objects in
ℛ
ID
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ID}}
during training and
ℛ
OOD
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{OOD}}
during evaluation.
The bimanual setting contains 38 tasks and tests a form of spatial generalization with no single-arm analogue: relocating the scene changes which arm must act and how the arms coordinate, requiring policies to solve held-out arm assignments, reversed handoff directions, and cross-body interactions (App.
E.3
).
MESA-Instance
tests generalization to unseen object instances with 20 single-arm and 18 bimanual tasks.
For each task, we train on one subset of object assets and evaluate on a disjoint held-out subset.
MESA-Composite
consists of unseen compositions of seen subtasks with 20 single-arm and 15 bimanual tasks.
These include tasks with familiar structure but novel object combinations and longer-horizon tasks with novel structure (e.g., two-step pick-and-place).
MESA-Category
consists of tasks involving object categories never seen during training with 20 single-arm and 17 bimanual tasks.
Policies must rely on broad vision-language understanding beyond the finetuning dataset.

(1) How do state-of-the-art RFMs and baseline policies perform under controlled distribution shifts?

5.2
Controlled Distribution Shifts
Since MESA-Spatial and MESA-Instance each contain tasks with in-distribution counterparts in MESA-ID, we can directly compare success rates with and without distribution shift (
Fig. 3

Across both spatial and instance-level shifts, performance generally drops relative to matched in-distribution tasks, indicating that MESA evaluates more than task memorization.

Because BiMESA-Spatial holds out the arm assignment rather than relocating objects corresponding to a single end-effector, it probes whether a policy has acquired

This suggests that current policies can often reuse reaching and object-transfer behaviors across arms, but struggle when cross-arm transfer requires precise contacts with articulated objects.

6
Conclusion and Limitations
We introduced MESA, a language-conditioned robotic manipulation benchmarking framework consisting of MESA-Gen, a scalable scene and data generation pipeline, and MESA-Bench, a fixed, reproducible benchmark.
Our experiments show that current RFMs leave substantial room for improvement in language following and out-of-distribution generalization, and that
MESA rankings are broadly consistent with real-world evaluation trends.
These results position MESA as a scalable testbed for measuring progress in general-purpose robotic manipulation.
A key limitation of MESA is its task distribution: most tasks involve pick-and-place or simple articulated manipulation, leaving more difficult settings such as dexterous, long-horizon, and mobile manipulation to future extensions.
Additionally, our language-following metric’s simplicity makes it robust at the cost of granularity, since it does not capture partial task completion or cases where a policy attempts the correct instruction but fails during execution.
MESA prioritizes controlled distribution shifts and accessible experimentation. Establishing how its method rankings and axis-specific findings carry over to real robots remains an important direction for future validation.
Rankings are conditional on the supplied training data and evaluation protocol; we have not tested whether alternative data sources, such as a matched human-teleoperation dataset, would preserve the ordering.

A.1
Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)
Why should I use MESA?
MESA enables quick iteration while giving strong signal about language steering and performance along our axes of generalization.
Furthermore, it is an easily extensible platform for designing new experimental settings.
What explains diffusion policy’s unexpectedly poor performance?
We study this question in depth in
App. B.1
.
To summarize, we find that our implementation works well in LIBERO and that DP simply struggles to fit the diverse setting presented by the benchmark.
What is this benchmark testing?
We designed this benchmark to test language steering and policy performance under changes in spatial arrangement, object instance, object category and with unseen subtask compositions.
BiMESA additionally tests bimanual generalization through held-out arm assignments, reversed handoff directions, and cross-arm articulated manipulation.
What does this benchmark not test for?
This benchmark is not geared towards dexterous or precise tasks, open-ended language steerability, long-horizon tasks or mobile manipulation.
What motivates the lack of scene and camera pose diversity?
We purposely limit diversity along axes we don’t test in order to minimize the amount of training required.
That being said, this is implemented and easy to add back if desired.
What are the limits of MimicGen data?
MimicGen data has several limitations, especially for our domain with such scene variety.
It is not well suited to deal with dense clutter, and even with our optimizations it sometimes outputs suboptimal trajectories.
We do not claim in this paper to present a general solution for robot data generation, or that our data is optimal.
Rather, we claim that MimicGen data is useful for inexpensively generating data for experiments, and that the stack ranking of policies trained on this data is broadly consistent with real-world rankings.
What types of tasks are included in the benchmark?
The benchmark mainly focuses on pick and place and articulated manipulation tasks.
This limited scope allows us to quickly generate data with MimicGen and train policies that achieve meaningful generalization under reasonable compute budgets.

Why Diffusion Policy Scales Poorly in Multi-Task MESA
One surprising finding is that, although we expected the diffusion policy to underperform relative to its VLA counterparts, it performs substantially worse than anticipated on the single-arm MESA benchmark, achieving an average success rate of 1.1%.
We investigate this in

A likely explanation is that, compared to the baselines using a VLM backbone, the DiT policy has a much weaker vision-language conditioning pathway and cannot adequately fit the diverse data included in the MESA dataset.

MimicGen works by initializing the environment, attempting to solve the task using its trajectory transformations, and then reinitializing it once it exceeds some retry budget.
This process can bias the dataset towards initial configurations where MimicGen is likely to succeed, creating issues for the downstream policy.
We study this issue by computing normalized initial position frequency for the single-arm setting in

We observe minor bias in the dataset with more data in the lower-right quadrant and fewer in the upper-left quadrant.
However, within each tile, the deviation from an unbiased distribution is at most about 15%, reflecting a relatively evenly distributed dataset.

C.1
Background: BDDL
The

[

44

allows users to specify task initial and goal states using logical predicates, and has been used for several high-profile benchmarks

More recently,
Saxena et al. [55]
adapts it to MimicGen, specifying the object-centric subtasks using demonstration predicates and enabling MimicGen-ready environment generation purely in the BDDL language.
C.2
Implemented Task Skeletons
We implement task skeletons as simple Python classes specifying task-relevant objects, initialization predicates, demonstration predicates, and goal predicates.
Our pipeline currently includes the
PickAndPlace
skeleton, which encompasses all pick-and-place tasks;
MultiStepArticulatedManipulation
, which encompasses tasks involving articulated objects, such as opening or closing them and placing objects in them; and, for the bimanual setting,
BimanualHandoff

D.3
Subtask Stitching for Unseen Tasks
While MimicGen is built to enable scalable data generation, it still requires human data collection for each task.
This requires linear human effort in the number of created tasks, which is cumbersome for researchers with limited resources.
To alleviate this, we use subtask stitching which, instead of using only demonstrations from the target task, uses MESA’s predicate structure to enable data generation for unseen tasks by composing object-centric subtasks sourced from other tasks with compatible predicates.
For example, for a target task “
pick up the apple and put it on the plate
”, MESA-Gen might combine the
Grasp(apple)
subtask from “
put the apple in the drawer
” with the
On(banana, plate)
subtask of “
put the banana on the plate
”.
Demonstration Lookup System:
We construct a hierarchical index offline by parsing all demonstration HDF5 files and extracting subtask-level metadata.
This index is organized primarily by predicate type (e.g.,
grasp, in, on, open, close, stack, turnon, turnoff
), and further refined by using object-centric semantic attributes such as shape category, object category, insertion type, destination region, and object identity.
The exact hierarchy depth and key structure depend on the predicate semantics; for example,
grasp
predicates are indexed by the grasped object’s shape and category, whereas
in
predicates additionally encode both the source object and destination container properties.
Each leaf node of the index contains a list of concrete subtask segments extracted from demonstrations, including the path to the source HDF5 file, the demonstration identifier, object references involved in the interaction, the subtask’s index within the original trajectory, and the termination signal defining the subtask boundary.
This is sufficient to recover the full state-action segment corresponding to the subtask and to re-contextualize it for a new task.
At generation time, we query this index using a predicate-specific set of search keys from the current subtask specification.
These keys are computed using object metadata functions that map object instances to semantic categories like shape class or insertion type.
Retrieval is performed by a recursive tree walk that follows the exact key if present.
If a key is not present, we take the union across all children at that depth, which broadens the search to semantically related subtasks.
From the retrieved candidate list, we select using either
RandomStrategy
or one of several nearest-neighbor strategies:
NearestNeighborObjectStrategy
(weighted object pose distance),
NearestNeighborRobotDistanceStrategy
(robot travel distance to the first transformed pose), or
NearestNeighborEefObjectTranslationStrategy
(relative end-effector-object translation).
Nearest-neighbor selection computes distances for all candidates and samples uniformly among the top-
k
k
closest (default
k
=
3
k=3
).
Stitching Procedure:
Each subtask is first trimmed from its source demonstration using start and end indices derived from predicate-specific termination signals stored in the HDF5 logs.
For non-final subtasks, boundaries are detected via the first valid rising edge of the corresponding terminal signal following the previous subtask; the final subtask extends to the end of the demonstration.
To increase diversity, subtask end boundaries may be randomly offset within a task-specified range, except for the final subtask, which is fixed to prevent truncation.
When concatenating subtasks, the system does not resample trajectories globally but instead inserts short transition segments to ensure temporal continuity.
Transitions optionally insert i) a linear interpolation segment that smoothly moves the end-effector from the final pose of the previous subtask to the initial pose of the next, and ii) a fix-pose hold segment that maintains the first pose of the next subtask for a fixed number of steps.
To avoid duplicate commands, the first waypoint of each appended segment is removed prior to concatenation.
After all subtasks are merged, their actions are concatenated into a single action sequence.
The environment is reset once at the beginning of the stitched trajectory, and all subsequent subtasks execute sequentially without intermediate resets.
We transform subtask trajectories to the current scene by preserving the relative pose between the end-effector and the reference object.
When objects differ in canonical rotation axes, an additional rotation correction is applied to align coordinate conventions.
Demonstrations with missing or invalid subtask termination signals are skipped during dataset parsing.
We enforce non-empty subtasks, strictly increasing subtask indices, and valid ordering of subtasks.
On generation, if no compatible subtasks are found even after using the broader semantic fallbacks mentioned previously, generation terminates.

Training and Auxiliary Datasets
Our main MESA-70 training set includes 7,000 demonstrations across the 70 tasks in MESA-70, which is about 1M frames.

E.5
Evaluation Details
To minimize bias caused by unlucky starting configurations, we generate a fixed set of starting configurations.
We use these configurations for all of our experiments and include them in our open-source code release.
Within each embodiment and task, rollout
i
i
restores the same object set and starting configuration for each method, ensuring comparisons use identical starting conditions.
When generating these starting configurations, we ensure that the target object is not occluded or out of frame and that the target task has not already been completed.
We evaluate each policy for 50 rollouts for each task, with each rollout consisting of a different set of distractor objects.
Note that since the distractor objects and initial positions are sampled completely independently of the training configurations,
every single initial observation in the benchmark is unique
, and solving this benchmark through memorization alone is infeasible.

Appendix F
Training Details for Baselines
Here we share additional details about our baselines.
F.1
Shared Details
Unless noted otherwise, all VLA / VLM-based policies except for GR00T are trained for 50k gradient steps with a batch size of 128. For GR00T, we use a batch size of 1024 as recommended by the authors on single-arm MESA and a batch size of 128 on BiMESA. The PaliGemma baselines and
π
\pi
models are trained using the JAX version of the official
openpi
repository, and GR00T is trained using the official
Isaac-GR00T
repository.
We use the default optimization parameters from these repositories for all experiments.
In the single-arm setting, all models are trained with a left shoulder camera image and a wrist camera image. The bimanual setup provides two wrist cameras and an egocentric camera.
All models are evaluated using the final model at the end of training.
All models use relative joint angle action space, which is a common choice in the pretraining data for our main VLA baselines.
Unless otherwise noted, all models are trained with a chunk size matching the control frequency of 20 Hz and we replan after executing the first 5 actions.
In general, we didn’t tune hyperparameters unless performance was surprisingly poor.
Policy inputs and privileged information.
During training and evaluation, policies are conditioned only on camera images, proprioception (joint positions and gripper widths), and the language instruction; demonstration actions provide the training targets.
No baseline receives ground-truth object poses, object-identity metadata, task predicates, or symmetry-derived grasp yaw as policy inputs.
Privileged simulator information is used by the demonstration-generation pipeline and by the evaluator to score task completion, not supplied to the policies.

F.3
Training Compute Usage
An important goal for our framework is to allow academic researchers to quickly iterate with minimal compute requirements.
We find that MESA’s dataset was small enough to achieve saturation after a relatively quick training cycle.
For the
openpi
repository models, the full 50k training steps took 48 hours on four L40S GPUs or 21 hours on two H100s.
We also saw that
π

\pi_{0.5}
achieved strong performance even after 5k gradient steps, and that it achieved strong performance with only 10% of our data, meaning it may be possible to shrink the budget even further.
For GR00T, the version with a batch size 128 took about 12 hours on one H100 and the version with batch size 1024 took about 30 hours with four H100s.
