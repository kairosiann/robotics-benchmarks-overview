Abstract
Deformable Linear Objects (DLOs), such as wires and cables, are central to industrial assembly. Unlike rigid objects, whose state is captured by a 6-DoF pose, DLOs have an infinite-dimensional configuration space and deform continuously under contact with grippers, fixtures, and the workspace, making them a demanding benchmark for general dexterous manipulation. Despite their importance, policy development and comparison remain difficult: existing benchmarks are often tied to specific hardware setups, lack modular and customizable task assets, or study generic deformable-object tasks without the fixtures relevant to real-world industrial wire manipulation. Few benchmarks align simulation, real-world data, and shared evaluation protocols. To bridge this gap, we introduce
WireCraft
, a simulation benchmark for industrial DLO manipulation with configurable difficulty and assets, spanning three task families: connector insertion, clip routing, and channel seating. It supports two complementary DLO physics models, articulated and deformable, and the released trajectories come from both simulation and a physical UR5. We benchmark reinforcement learning (RL), imitation learning (IL), and vision-language-action (VLA) policies under shared metrics. Privileged state-based RL solves a representative setting in each task family with over 82% success, confirming the tasks are well-posed. Image-based policies remain less successful across the evaluated tasks, with ACT and DiT VLA falling below privileged State PPO. On a physical UR5, supplementing real demonstrations with simulated data improves success by 15–65 percentage points in the best-performing mixture for each task. These results indicate that industrial DLO manipulation, though tractable under privileged state, remains an open challenge for current vision-based learning.
Our project is open-sourced at
https://github.com/DORLR/WireCraft

To address this gap, we make the following contributions:
•
We introduce WireCraft, a unified simulation benchmark for industrial DLO manipulation across connector insertion, clip routing, and channel seating, with configurable task components, standardized policy interfaces, and two complementary DLO physics models trading off speed and fidelity.
•
We evaluate representative RL, IL, and VLA policies under a shared evaluation protocol, providing a consistent reference for future work on industrial DLO learning. Simulation baseline evaluation and physical UR5 validation cover all three task families.
•
We open-source the benchmark with available trajectory data, data-generation tools, and 3D-printable task-board components. This enables researchers to reproduce the setups, collect demonstrations, and study sim-to-real transfer for industrial DLO manipulation.

Learning algorithms for contact-rich and deformable manipulation.
Recent progress in robot learning has produced several policy classes relevant to contact-rich manipulation, including on-policy and off-policy reinforcement learning

3
WireCraft Infrastructure
Figure 2:
WireCraft Architecture
, including the WireCraft simulation engine, asset library, task suite, demonstration sources, policy baselines, and real-world validation setup.
WireCraft supports articulated and FEM-based deformable wire models, provides configurable industrial DLO manipulation tasks across connector insertion, clip routing, and channel seating, and pairs simulation data with real-world teleoperation trajectories under a shared dataset schema.
3.1
WireCraft Engine
Simulation Backend.
WireCraft is built on Isaac Lab v2.2.1
[
34
]
, an open-source robot learning framework on top of Isaac Sim 4.5
[
37
]
; full version and physics details are in Appendix
B
. Isaac Sim provides photo-realistic scenes and fast simulation, while Isaac Lab supports GPU-accelerated, multi-instance training for scalable policy learning and trajectory generation.
DLO Physics.
WireCraft simulates its two wire representations through complementary physics mechanisms. The articulated wire uses rigid-body dynamics: each segment is a rigid body, and neighboring segments are coupled by 6-DoF joints (PhysX D6). This supports high-throughput trajectory generation due to efficient rigid-body contact but can become unstable under adjacent-segment collision or accumulated contact forces. The deformable wire uses Isaac Sim’s deformable body API and PhysX FEM deformable body simulation
[
21
,
36
]
, improving local bending, compression, and contact deformation with higher fidelity, but at higher computational cost. We treat the two wire types as complementary benchmark representations. Detailed wire-model parameters and throughput measurements are reported in Appendix
B
, and a wire–connector physics visualization is shown in Figure
6
. Details of DLO and scene interaction are provided in Appendix
B.3
.
Sensors.
Beyond standard camera RGB data, WireCraft provides built-in interfaces to optional depth channels. To capture physical interactions, the platform integrates contact sensors for precise collision detection and force sensors to monitor grasping force magnitudes and insertion states. Furthermore, real-time robot proprioception, including joint positions and velocities, is natively supported. Observation inputs are selected per baseline, and users can add other sensor types supported by the NVIDIA Isaac ecosystem.

DLO Assets.
The articulated wires are parameterized by length, radius, colors, damping, and stiffness. The deformable wires are simulated via FEM, where Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and density govern its elastic response, alongside geometric parameters for length, radius, and mesh resolution. Both share the same connector and task-component interfaces, so all tasks can be instantiated with either wire type.
Connector Assets.
DLO-mounted connectors are 3D-printable and span two families: reference-based connectors modeled after real standards such as USB-A, and geometry-based connectors built from primitives such as cylinders and cuboids. They differ in rotational symmetry, allowing task difficulty to range from rotation-invariant to orientation-sensitive insertion.
Fixture Assets.
Task-side fixtures, including clips, channels, and sockets, are likewise 3D-printable. They define the geometric constraints of each task family and remain configurable for different layouts and difficulty levels.
Robot Embodiments.
Three simulated embodiments are supported: the UR5, Franka, and Trossen Stationary AI, each with its own gripper and control interface. Tasks can be instantiated across different arms under a unified observation framework, where robot-specific proprioception scales to each robot’s degrees of freedom.
4
WireCraft Benchmark
4.1
WireCraft Tasks
We benchmark three industrial task families.
Connector Insertion
performs precision contact-rich insertion with a trailing deformable wire;
Clip Routing
performs fixture-constrained routing under contact and topological constraints; and
Channel Seating
performs continuous shape control along extended geometric constraints.
Task Curriculum.
Task difficulty varies through connector geometry, fixture complexity, and path geometry. Connector insertion ranges from a rotation-invariant cylindrical connector to an asymmetric, orientation-sensitive DisplayPort-style connector. Clip routing difficulty scales with the number and placement of clips, while channel seating difficulty is controlled by channel curvature.
Randomization.
All tasks share a randomized free-fall wire initialization, where the wire is spawned with varying position and orientation. Task-specific randomization varies the socket position for insertion, clip position and orientation for routing, and channel geometry for seating; detailed ranges are provided in Appendix
C.4
.
4.2
WireCraft Demonstrations
Data Collection.
WireCraft supports four data collection methods that share a common dataset schema. Scripted policies provide scalable, successful demonstrations using privileged simulator state; RL rollouts add closed-loop interaction data and failure-adjacent states; human teleoperation in simulation captures corrective visual strategies for contact-rich manipulation; and real-world UR5 demonstrations provide physical validation. Our data-collection stack builds on an open-source ROS 2 + LeRobot teleoperation stack for WireCraft, with a shared episode recorder for simulation and real-robot data.
Shared Dataset Schema.
All sources are serialized into a LeRobot v3.0 dataset format. For the UR5 robot, they include a 7-D state (six joint positions and gripper), a 7-D action (six joint commands and gripper), synchronized wrist and third-person RGB streams at the task control rate, and the language instruction for each task. This shared schema allows IL and VLA policies to be trained from any mixture of these sources without policy-specific task wrappers.

Real-World Validation
For real-world validation, we evaluate all three WireCraft tasks on a physical UR5 using ACT, which achieved the highest insertion success rate among image-input baselines in Section
5
. We first reproduce the physical setup in simulation, then train ACT on four combinations of real demonstrations (R), scripted simulation demonstrations (S), and simulation RL rollouts (P): R, R+S, R+P, and R+S+P. All configurations train for 80k steps, with checkpoints selected by the lowest L1 loss on held-out real demonstrations. Preliminary simulation-only policies showed poor zero-shot transfer, so we focus the quantitative evaluation on mixtures containing real demonstrations.
The hardware evaluation uses a narrower randomization range than simulation. For insertion, the connector is placed within a
3
×
3
3\times 3
cm region with varying tilt, while the socket remains fixed. For 1-clip routing, a single clip is randomly positioned near the wire on both sides. For straight-channel seating, one wire endpoint remains fixed while the other is placed at varying positions outside the channel. We evaluate each task and data-source combination over 20 real-robot trials, and report different effects of simulation data across tasks in Figure

For insertion, reach success remains between 80% and 95% across data combinations, while insertion success increases from 15% with R to 80% with R+S+P. Higher reach success does not necessarily translate into higher insertion success: R+P reaches more often than R+S+P (95% vs. 85%), but inserts less often (65% vs. 80%). Qualitatively, R+S+P more often produces insertion-compatible grasp poses, whereas R+P sometimes reaches the socket with a grasp that leaves the connector poorly aligned for insertion. The benefit of broader variability of data coverage is therefore associated with grasping the connector in a pose that supports subsequent socket alignment. Successful pickup and reach alone are insufficient.
Clip routing also benefits from simulated data, but the improvement is non-monotonic: success increases from 45% with R to 65% with R+P, while R+S+P achieves 60%. Qualitatively, failures with simulated training data more often place the wire on top of the clip opening instead of beside the clip. These near-misses remain unsuccessful under the binary criterion but indicate closer placement alignment. Clip localization during wire grasping relies on the side-camera view alone, leaving limited visual information for preparing the subsequent placement.
For channel seating, simulated data increase success from 75% with R to 90% with R+P and R+S+P. Most failures occur during the initial grasp; once the wire is successfully grasped, seating is usually completed. Its remaining bottleneck is therefore grasp acquisition, unlike the final alignment and placement failures observed in insertion and clip routing.
Overall, simulated trajectories improve the observed success rates across all three tasks, with task-dependent effects on grasp selection and fixture engagement. The hardware results support the portability of the three task definitions under restricted initialization ranges. Since source diversity and dataset size increase together, these comparisons show the benefit of supplementing real demonstrations with simulated trajectories, without isolating the effect of diversity alone. Further setup and failure details are provided in Appendix
H
.
7
Limitations
WireCraft has several limitations. First, policy transfer between the articulated and FEM-based wire models has not been evaluated, and their dynamic deformation and contact response remain quantitatively unmatched. Neither model has been calibrated against the physical wire, so hardware task validation does not establish wire-model accuracy or reliable zero-shot transfer. Second, full force/torque feedback remains unused: privileged insertion uses only saturated fingertip contact magnitudes, while the UR5’s mounted force/torque sensor is unread. Simulated contact forces remain physically unvalidated, and the benefits of force/torque feedback and optional depth observations for task completion are untested. Third, hardware evaluation covers all three task families but only selected settings on one UR5, leaving generalization across robots, fixtures, and wire materials untested. Success metrics also do not capture contact loads or wire damage. Finally, the VLA results reflect constrained fine-tuning and do not establish an upper bound on VLA capability; longer training, improved checkpoint selection, and stronger visual adaptation remain future work.
8
Conclusion
We presented WireCraft, a simulation benchmark for industrial DLO manipulation across connector insertion, clip routing, and channel seating. WireCraft provides configurable 3D-printable assets, articulated and FEM-based wire representations, shared policy interfaces, and trajectory data from scripted, RL, and real-world sources, together with teleoperation tools. Our experiments show a clear gap between task feasibility and current vision-based policy performance. Privileged state-based RL solves the evaluated insertion, routing, and seating settings, indicating that the tasks are well posed in simulation. In contrast, vision-based RL, imitation learning, and VLA policies degrade sharply on connector insertion, while ACT and DiT VLA also fall below State PPO on routing and seating. The reach–insert gap shows that reaching the socket neighborhood is not sufficient: fine alignment, occlusion, deformation, and contact-rich seating remain the central bottlenecks. Real-world UR5 validation covers all three task families and shows improved ACT success rates when real demonstrations are supplemented with simulated trajectories.
By releasing WireCraft, we aim to provide a common substrate for studying where industrial DLO policies fail and how they improve, including visual localization, deformation-aware control, contact-rich insertion, fixture-constrained routing, and sim-to-real transfer.

B.2
DLO Models
WireCraft provides two complementary wire representations for the same benchmark tasks. They are not intended to be parameter-by-parameter calibrated material matches, since the articulated chain and FEM soft body expose different solver-native parameters. Instead, they are aligned at the task level through shared wire endpoints, connector interfaces, fixtures, observation-action schemas, and success criteria. The articulated wire is a chain of rigid capsule segments connected by 6-DoF joints (PhysX D6 joints) whose angular degrees of freedom are driven by passive implicit spring–damper actuators, simulated under rigid-body dynamics. This representation supports high-throughput trajectory generation but can become unstable under adjacent-segment self-contact or accumulated contact forces. The deformable wire is a PhysX FEM soft body: a slender triangulated cylindrical rod (decagonal cross-section, 10 radial × 40 axial subdivisions; length 0.4 m, radius 3 mm) that PhysX voxelizes into a hexahedral FEM simulation mesh (resolution 40 along the major axis). This captures local bending, compression, and contact deformation at higher fidelity but greater cost. The parameter names reflect each model’s native representation and should not be read as physically equivalent material settings.

Appendix C
Task Design
C.1
Connector Insertion
Connector insertion requires the robot to grasp the rigid connector at the end of a deformable wire and insert it into a matching socket. The task combines local peg-in-hole precision with global deformation effects: the trailing wire introduces drag, gravity-induced shape changes, and contact disturbances that perturb the connector during final alignment.
The task curriculum is set by connector and socket geometry, which is fixed per task rather than randomized across episodes. WireCraft registers five connector/socket geometries that span a range of rotational symmetries and alignment sensitivities. The cylinder connector requires the held connector’s long axis to align with the socket axis but is rotationally symmetric about that axis, making it the easiest variant. The cuboid connector extends the cylinder requirement and additionally requires alignment of the rotation about the long axis. The Ethernet-style connector adds a latch-like geometry and serves as our primary insertion task; the DisplayPort connector has an asymmetric shell and is the most alignment-sensitive; and the USB-A connector provides a reference-standard geometry. Among these, the cylinder, cuboid, Ethernet-style, and DisplayPort connectors are used in the benchmark evaluations (Table 1); the USB-A connector is released as an additional asset but is not included in the reported experiments. A second difficulty axis is the socket clearance, which controls the gap between connector and socket; a smaller clearance yields a tighter, harder insertion.
C.2
Clip Routing
Clip routing requires the robot to route a DLO through one or more structured U-slot clips mounted on the workspace. The task emphasizes fixture interaction and topological constraints: the DLO must be guided into the clip openings while maintaining contact stability and avoiding incomplete seating or slippage.
The task curriculum is set by the number of clips, fixed per task. Difficulty scales from single-clip routing to multi-clip sequences of up to three clips, where additional clips lengthen the multi-step route and require intermediate re-grasps. Each clip’s
x
x
position is fixed along the routing direction, while its
y
y
position and yaw are randomized per episode. A secondary difficulty axis is the clip opening: the top-gap and lip retention can be adjusted, and a sufficiently wide opening removes the lip and reduces the clip to an unconstrained U-slot.
C.3
Channel Seating
Channel seating requires the robot to lay a DLO into a geometric channel so that the DLO body conforms to the target path. Unlike connector insertion, where success depends on a local terminal pose, channel seating evaluates extended shape control over the entire DLO body.
The task curriculum is set by channel geometry, fixed per task. Each channel is constructed from five segments, and difficulty is controlled by the random relative rotation applied at each segment-to-segment joint: the straight channel uses zero inter-segment rotation and tests simple alignment along a linear path, while the curved channel applies a per-joint random bend of up to
±
20
∘
\pm 20^{\circ}
, producing a non-linear path that requires continuous path-following.
C.4

Each task applies per-episode randomization at reset, on top of the fixed per-task curriculum described above. Connector insertion randomizes the wire-attached connector and the socket; clip routing randomizes the clips; and channel seating randomizes the wire-attached connector and, optionally, the channel shape. Appendix D
Scripted Policy
All three task families share a common scripted-policy framework: a per-environment vectorized finite-state machine that, within each phase, reads the current end-effector and fixture poses, expresses a target end-effector pose in the robot base frame, and emits a Cartesian delta-pose action obtained by clamping the pose error toward that target. Each policy selects the control mode best suited to its task (see Appendix
C.5
). A phase advances once the end-effector reaches its target within a position and orientation tolerance and dwells for a minimum number of control steps. At the grasp phase the policy snapshots the relevant poses, so that every post-grasp phase servos toward a stable reference that is unaffected by the deformation introduced once the wire is held. D.1
Insertion
The insertion policy grasps the connector at its tail and seats it into the socket. After grasping and lifting, it moves to a pre-insertion pose, solves for the gripper orientation that aligns the held connector with the socket under a tightened orientation tolerance, drives the connector into the socket, and releases. To produce demonstrations of failure and recovery, with probability
0.3
0.3
the policy injects a pick-and-drop perturbation, lifting and releasing the connector mid-air before re-approaching and regrasping, and a failure-triggered retry returns to the approach phase if the insertion has not succeeded after the hold phase, for up to two attempts. Per-episode randomization of the approach pitch and the tail grasp offset, together with phase-local slowdowns around the pick and insertion, further diversify the demonstrations.
D.2
Clip Routing
The clip-routing policy grasps the deformable wire itself at the node nearest the clip, rather than the connector, since the connector is wider than the clip mouth and cannot enter the slot. The grasp node is located by proximity and the grasp orientation is set to the local wire tangent. After descending to a fixed table-relative height and closing on the wire, the policy lifts and rotates the held wire to the clip’s slot axis. It then over-stretches the wire by translating past the placement target along the anchor-to-clip direction, pulls back to the clip front to release excess tension, and pushes the held wire down past the clip lip so that it seats into the slot under a controlled descent. Success requires a wire node to lie inside the clip slot. The anchor-to-clip direction is frozen at grasp time because the wire’s far endpoint is a dynamic body that drifts under tension, which would otherwise cause the placement target to move every step.
D.3
Channel Seating
The channel-seating policy grasps the connector and lays the wire into the channel so that its body follows the target path. Unlike the other two policies, which use full Cartesian control, it uses the fixed-orientation Cartesian mode with a much finer action scale to keep the connector level and produce smooth, non-snagging motion. It grasps the connector gently, holding the gripper open for a settling delay before closing, then lifts vertically and settles. It then descends along the channel path at a strongly reduced speed, seating the wire at the channel-center height before releasing. Following the path rather than descending at a single waypoint lets the same policy handle both straight and curved channels.

F.3
Checkpoint Selection
Each policy was checkpointed periodically during training and evaluated using the original reach-success criterion. We retained the top three checkpoints for each policy according to this reach success, rather than selecting checkpoints using insert success after the final evaluation. This avoids choosing checkpoints based on the stricter test metric while still allowing us to report how the best training-time checkpoints behave under both reach and insert success criteria. Because checkpoints are selected by reach success on the evaluation rollouts themselves rather than on a held-out validation set, the reported figures are an optimistic, best-observed estimate under our training budget and should not be read as validation-based model selection; insert success is taken from the same checkpoint and is therefore not separately optimized.

Appendix H
Real-World Validation
We evaluate ACT on a physical UR5 across connector insertion, 1-clip routing, and straight-channel seating. For each task, we compare four training-data combinations: real demonstrations (R), scripted simulation demonstrations (S), and simulation RL rollouts (P), combined as R, R+S, R+P, and R+S+P. Preliminary trials showed poor zero-shot performance for simulation-only policies, so we restrict the full hardware evaluation to data combinations containing real demonstrations. The comparison examines whether simulated trajectories supplement real demonstrations under shared task definitions and policy interfaces.
H.1
Setup
Physical setup.
The platform uses a UR5 arm with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper and two RealSense D405 cameras providing side and wrist RGB views. The fixtures include a 3D-printed Ethernet-style connector and socket, a single clip, and a straight channel. Connector insertion and clip routing use a 6 mm-diameter HDMI cable, while channel seating uses a 10 mm-diameter green cord with red and yellow flecks. A force–torque sensor is mounted between the arm flange and gripper, but its readings are not used by ACT. We reproduce its end-effector offset in simulation and match the task-board appearance and camera poses to the physical setup. ACT observes RGB images and robot proprioception, but not depth and force/torque inputs, and executes action commands at 30 Hz.

H.3
Results and Analysis
Representative failure modes are shown in Figure
11
.
Connector insertion.
Observed failures include unsuccessful pickup and reaching without seating. The gripper sometimes closes beside the connector or fails to secure it during lifting. After successful pickup, the connector may reach the socket but remain unseated because of its position or orientation within the grasp. A grasp sufficient for transport may therefore be unsuitable for insertion.
Clip routing.
The wire may be released beside the clip opening or rest on the clip rim without entering the slot. In these cases, the gripper reaches the fixture, but the wire remains incorrectly positioned relative to the opening.
Channel seating.
Missed grasps leave the free endpoint on the board. After successful pickup, moving the endpoint near the channel can still leave part of the wire outside it; successful seating requires placing the wire body within the channel.

Limitations.
The hardware study covers three tasks on one UR5 with restricted initialization ranges and 20 trials per configuration. Indistinguishable difference in count of successful trials cannot lead to a definitive ranking of data mixtures. Source composition and dataset size vary together, so these comparisons measure data supplementation without isolating source diversity from data quantity.
