01 / The central distinction

A task count is not a capability map.

These benchmarks overlap heavily in picking, placing, opening, closing, and sequencing actions. Their real differences are what changes between trials, what the robot must remember, how realistic the interaction is, and what evidence counts as success.

A hundred variants of a placement problem can diagnose generalization better than ten unrelated tasks. A thousand household activity definitions do not mean a thousand standardized leaderboard evaluations. And a high simulated success rate is evidence about real deployment only when the relationship has been tested. This is the guide’s synthesis, rather than a claim made jointly by the authors.

Learning & transfer

LIBERO asks whether new skills build on old knowledge without erasing it. RoboCasa365 expands this to diverse kitchen tasks, environments, pretraining, and adaptation.

Validity & realism

SIMPLER asks whether simulation preserves real policy rankings. BEHAVIOR asks whether robots can accomplish useful, extended household activities with richer object states.

Physical competence

DataCenterBench tests camera-driven agents on physical controls and connections. RoboDojo probes complementary manipulation capabilities in simulation and on real robots.

How to use this guide

Read the comparison first, then each benchmark’s evaluation walkthrough. The task explorer contains the full retrieved inventories, including exact LIBERO and BEHAVIOR goals and published RoboDojo / DataCenterBench scoring rubrics. Search a skill, object, or proposed task and pin results to compare the available setup and scoring evidence. Embedded setup images are available offline. External media loads when you open it.

Four terms that prevent misleading comparisons

Task: the requested behavior or formal goal. Instance: a particular scene, object selection, and initial state for that task. More instances measure reliability without necessarily adding new skills.

Episode / trial: one attempt from reset to termination. Suite / split: a grouping designed to isolate a capability or separate training from evaluation. Two percentages are comparable only after these choices are aligned.

03 / Knowledge transfer

LIBERO

Learn the next task.
Keep the earlier ones.

NeurIPS 2023 · robosuite / MuJoCo · Franka arm

LIBERO separates declarative knowledge—which object and spatial relation an instruction refers to—from procedural knowledge—how to physically achieve it. Its original question concerns lifelong robot learning, where a policy encounters tasks in sequence rather than training on everything jointly.

How an attempt is evaluated

1Reset an instance

Load the task’s BDDL definition and a supplied initial simulator state.

2Execute the instruction

The policy uses image observations and robot state to command the gripper and arm.

3Check formal goals

The environment checks predicates such as On, In, Open, or Turnon, including conjunctions.

4Average trial outcomes

An episode succeeds when the goal is met within its rollout horizon. The rollout wrapper determines termination details.

Worked example: “Put the black bowl in the bottom drawer … and close it” requires the bowl-inside condition and drawer-closed condition together. Merely grasping the bowl or placing it in an open drawer does not complete the task. Exact task predicates appear in the explorer; their physical tolerances live in the simulator predicate implementations.

What the original paper measures beyond success rate

For each task, the paper trains for 50 epochs on its 50 demonstrations, evaluates every five epochs using 20 rollout trajectories with maximum length 600, and repeats the learning experiment over seeds. It reports forward transfer (FWT), which summarizes how readily the current task is acquired; negative backward transfer (NBT), which measures degradation on earlier tasks; and area under the success-rate curve (AUC), which combines acquisition and subsequent retention. Lower NBT and higher FWT / AUC are better. The paper’s FWT is a learning-curve measure, not simply zero-shot performance on a new task. Source: paper, evaluation metrics and experimental details.

Interpretation trap

A modern model’s single final LIBERO success percentage does not establish resistance to catastrophic forgetting. That claim requires sequential training and re-evaluating earlier tasks. Horizon, reset states, number of trials, and task ordering must also be specified.

RoboCasa
& RoboCasa365

Generalization across tasks, kitchens, objects, and training regimes.

RSS 2024 → ICLR 2026 · robosuite / MuJoCo

The original RoboCasa framework scales up realistic kitchen environments, interactable fixtures, and demonstrations. RoboCasa365 turns that foundation into a larger suite for multi-task learning, foundation-model pretraining and adaptation, and lifelong learning. A mobile manipulator must often move between fixtures while tracking a multi-step instruction.

Which RoboCasa task set?

The 2024 paper describes 25 atomic and 75 composite tasks. The archived v0.2 documentation retrieved for this guide lists 25 atomic and 86 composite rows; that later catalog is preserved separately in the explorer and should not be called the original paper’s exact 100-task list. RoboCasa365 has a complete 365-entry catalog here, including instructions, activity, mobile-manipulation requirement, and subtask counts. Archived v0.2 catalogs · Current task metadata.

Atomic tasks cover pick-and-place, doors, drawers and racks, faucet controls, stove knobs, coffee-machine interactions, microwave controls, toaster / toaster-oven controls, electric kettles, blender lids / operation, stand-mixer heads, oven preheating, and navigation. Composite tasks combine these into activities such as preparing food and beverages, serving, washing, organizing, packing, and cleaning. Read the actual instruction: a task titled “Cut Buffet Pizza” asks for retrieving a pizza cutter, rather than simulating the full act of cutting pizza.

Three evaluation regimes answer different questions

Multi-task learning

Train a language-conditioned policy on pretraining demonstrations across 300 tasks: 65 atomic + 235 composite. Evaluate target splits to assess both familiar tasks and compositional transfer.

Foundation-model learning

Pretrain on broader data, then fine-tune on target-task demonstrations. A target labeled “unseen” during pretraining can become seen during adaptation; always state the data-access regime.

Lifelong learning

Introduce task data in stages and measure both learning and retention. Its evidence differs from jointly training on the complete data pool.

How tasks are evaluated

The paper’s target set contains 18 Atomic, 16 Composite-Seen, and 16 Composite-Unseen tasks. Composite-Unseen is zero-shot with respect to benchmark task-specific pretraining data in the multi-task experiment; it is not a universal claim that no underlying foundation model has seen similar behavior. The paper evaluates each task with 30 trials, a task-specific maximum duration, and a binary success condition checked during execution. Success rates are averaged across tasks. Target task tables · Appendix G: evaluation protocol.

Worked example: a “PickPlaceCounterToCabinet” task requires the object to reach its cabinet target under the task checker. A multi-step “LoadDishwasher” task asks for counter items to be put in the dishwasher and the door closed. The source environment’s _check_success() defines what the benchmark actually tests; success is not inferred from the task title or a video judge. The explorer embeds the executable success check for all 365 tasks, resolving inherited implementations and linking each task class to a pinned source snapshot. Helper functions define any referenced tolerances. Training subtask labels are useful diagnostic supervision but do not turn the primary paper metric into a partial-credit score.

A material protocol update

The official project reports that v1.0.1 increased task horizons by 1.5× on May 12, 2026. Paper-era numbers and current runs therefore need version and horizon labels. The live multi-task docs also report updated training durations / data hours. Use the paper for its experimental claims and the pinned release for reproduction. Official update log.

Two ways to manage the simulation gap

Visual Matching

Use real background images and tune simulated foreground appearance to resemble the real camera view. Align robot control so the same action has a comparable physical effect. This tries to reduce mismatch for a specific setup.

Variant Aggregation

Evaluate across simulated background, lighting, texture, and distractor variations, then aggregate results. Instead of depending on a single perfectly matched view, test whether policy comparisons survive nuisance variation.

How a task—and the evaluator itself—is judged

Task level: run a policy over the prescribed initial robot / object configurations and read environment success checks, such as drawer joint state, object proximity, stable stacking, or placement. Aggregate success across the evaluation grid and, when using Variant Aggregation, across environment variants. The official scripts encode the pose sweeps, horizons, and policy-specific settings; there is no single trial count covering every task and both protocols. Evaluation scripts.

Evaluator level: compare each policy’s simulated score with its measured real score on the matched task. The paper reports Pearson correlation and introduces Mean Maximum Rank Violation (MMRV). For each policy, MMRV finds the largest real-performance gap to a policy that simulation ranks incorrectly relative to it, then averages across policies. Lower is better. It penalizes reversing a large real-world gap more than reversing a near tie. Pearson measures linear agreement; MMRV focuses on consequential ranking mistakes. Paper: evaluation pipeline metrics.

Worked example

Two policies can both obtain 70% simulated drawer success while differing in real performance. Success rate measures the robot’s behavior in the simulator; MMRV measures whether that simulator makes useful policy-selection decisions. Conversely, correct ranking can coexist with biased absolute success rates. These are distinct claims.

Limits: the evidence is strongest for the real setups, policy families, and task distributions studied. It does not establish that an arbitrary new simulation task predicts real performance, or that matching backgrounds eliminates contact-dynamics error.

Project example: RT-2-X places an apple into a top drawer. More policy / task videos.

06 / Useful household work

BEHAVIOR

Activities with object states,
not just object poses.

BEHAVIOR-100 → BEHAVIOR-1K · iGibson → OmniGibson

BEHAVIOR starts from everyday activities and human demand for robot assistance. The original 100-activity benchmark uses time-use data; BEHAVIOR-1K expands to 1,000 activities informed by human surveys. Its technical ambition is to represent tasks that involve much more than rearrangement: washing, cooking, cutting, soaking, filling, folding, and navigating realistic homes.

BEHAVIOR-100 paper · BEHAVIOR-1K paper · Current project

Three sets that should not be confused

BEHAVIOR-100

The original 100 activity definitions, evaluated in iGibson 2.0. The explorer includes all 100 names and formal goals from the legacy BDDL registry, linked to their exact specifications.

BEHAVIOR-1K library

The paper describes 1,000 activities. The current official knowledgebase snapshot contains 1,016 task definitions, all included here with exact logical goals. A named activity can have an explicitly numbered definition.

2026 challenge

A selected, standardized set of 100 full-length tasks, rather than an evaluation of the whole library. The explorer labels these and includes the gallery’s scene-specific instruction and demo link.

What is in the full inventory?

The library spans food preparation and storage; appliance operation; dishwashing; room and surface cleaning; laundry and clothing; organization and packing; waste and recycling; household maintenance; and other indoor / outdoor chores. These are reading aids, not an invented official partition. The explorer keeps the official names, object synsets, required predicate types, definition identifiers, and goals instead of reducing a thousand activities to a few examples. Official full task catalog.

How one task becomes an evaluable episode

A BDDL activity defines objects, initial conditions, and a goal formula. Sampling binds symbolic objects to physical assets in a compatible scene, then creates a valid starting state. The simulator’s state predicates map geometry and extended physical states—such as inside, covered, soaked, cooked, or toggled on—to truth values. The agent’s physical actions change these states; the goal evaluator determines completion. Quantifiers and alternatives can represent multiple valid solutions. Official concepts · Task definitions.

Worked example: adding chemicals to a lawn is not complete because the agent holds a bottle near the grass. The retrieved goal requires the lawn to be covered with the specified fertilizer and herbicide. The source’s predicates decide whether those conditions hold. For a task with several objects, the goal may quantify over all required items; moving one object can earn partial progress while leaving full completion false.

Paper evaluation versus current challenge

The BEHAVIOR-1K paper introduces the broad activity / asset system and evaluates baseline learning on a selected subset; it is not evidence that those baselines were tested on every activity. Its analyses include success and efficiency such as navigation distance, simulation time, and object disarrangement. The 2026 challenge instead ranks by the task success score Q, averaged across its 100 tasks. The rules describe partial success as the fraction of goal BDDL predicates satisfied at the end of an episode; fully satisfied goals earn full credit. Paper experiments · Current challenge rules.

Challenge Q: average over tasks of goal-satisfaction scores; full success is stricter than nonzero partial credit.

For complex quantified / disjunctive goals, rely on the BDDL evaluator’s grounding and logical treatment rather than counting lines in the BDDL file. Secondary challenge metrics are simulated time, distance navigated, and hand / end-effector displacement, normalized using human demonstration averages; they break ranking ties. The evaluator also has explicit task-specific rollout and accounted-time budgets. Current rules exempt the first policy query’s duration, but charge subsequent policy query time and allocated simulation time; over-budget / failed rollouts receive zero. These operational rules matter for slow reasoning agents. Evaluation and efficiency accounting.

Interpretation trap

A high average partial score can reflect many almost-finished activities while full-task completion remains low. Report both. Rich simulation also does not independently prove reliable real-world cooking, cleaning, deformable manipulation, or safety.

Challenge example: move three soda cans from the living room to a kitchen trash can. All 100 challenge videos.

07 / Real controls and connections

DataCenterBench

Can a multimodal agent perform a basic physical procedure step?

Tier 1 · October 28, 2026 · real bimanual hardware

Robocurve takes steps from data-center equipment procedures and rebuilds them as resettable tabletop tasks. Frontier models observe cameras, issue end-effector tool calls, and act on controls or connectors. The benchmark tests closed-loop perception and physical action through an agent harness, including the practical effects of latency.

Release report · Methodology · Task instructions, resets, rubrics & photos

10single-step tasks
3tries per model per task
40model-call budget per try

The complete Tier 1 task set

Six control tasks: turn knob to mark; turn key; flip switch; lift protective cover; type code; move slider. Four connection tasks: plug charger; unplug charger; join Ethernet connectors; press cable into clip. Five use binary scoring; five allow half credit. Each has fixed physical props, start state, instruction, and rubric. The explorer includes every rubric verbatim, plus the setup and reset description.

Evaluation walkthrough

  1. Reset the same rig against reference photos. Each task uses one rig. Two I2RT YAM arms operate at a quarter of full speed. Models receive an overhead and two wrist-camera images at 224 × 224 after each step, retaining the last two steps’ images.
  2. Run until completion claim, 40 model calls, or a robot stop. There is no fixed trial wall-time limit. A motor exceeding 65 °C triggers a thermal stop; the starting cooldown requirement prevents heat from a previous trial being attributed to the next model.
  3. Grade observed outcome, not the agent’s claim. Two LLM judges independently score full-resolution before / after photographs using the instruction and rubric. Agreement is accepted; disagreement or “unsure” goes to a human. The keypad’s keypress log determines exact / one-edit-away credit, cross-checked by judges.
  4. Count model-caused failures. Giving up, call-limit exhaustion, malformed calls, refusal, dislodging props, and in-trial overheating still count. External camera, network, power, or similar faults can be set aside and rerun under the stated rules.

Source for the complete protocol: official methodology.

How the score is formed

runₖ = mean score on 10 tasks in counted try k
avg@3 = (run₁ + run₂ + run₃) / 3

The report displays a 95% bootstrap interval from resampling tasks and exports standard error across the three runs. Cost is estimated from token counts and dated API prices, including the serving tiers used; duration runs from the first model call until the arms begin parking. These are system-level measurements, affected by model inference speed, harness, cameras, motion settings, and hardware. Aggregation, uncertainty, cost, and duration definitions.

Worked example: on the knob task, pointing the knob’s line at the goal mark earns 1, a noticeable clockwise movement that stops short earns ½, and no clockwise progress earns 0. An overheat does not automatically erase partial physical progress: the trial is scored at its stopping outcome.

What the result does and does not establish

These are simplified component motions, not full data-center maintenance jobs. The release excludes electrical isolation, termination, instrument testing, and fault-finding; it has not validated transfer to real equipment forces or access constraints. Three tries per task provide limited ranking resolution, and a single rig per task entangles task difficulty with setup-specific effects. Release limitations.

Separate memory, precision, generalization, and execution failures.

Simulation evaluation

The paper evaluates each of 42 tasks for 50 episodes, totaling 2,100. For the 12 Generalization tasks, those are split into 25 standard and 25 randomized episodes. Randomization varies backgrounds, clutter, relevant objects, and lighting. Simulation uses the ARX X5 bimanual platform; heterogeneous parallel execution runs different scenes / tasks concurrently. The reported metrics are success rate and average partial score. The overall result is the mean of the five dimension-level means, so the 12 Generalization tasks do not outweigh the six Memory tasks. Paper, benchmark settings.

Overall = (Generalization + Memory + Precision + Long-Horizon + Open) / 5

Worked example: the simulated “Insert Tubes” rubric gives 0 if no tube is inserted; 20 for one correctly inserted tube; 40 for two; and 100 for all three plus returning the robot to origin. Required depth, upright orientation, and gripper release matter. Another task can have a different rubric: “Align Blocks” is all-or-nothing and forbids lifting cubes above an allowed height. The explorer exposes these task-specific requirements rather than assuming a universal fraction-of-objects score. Tube scoring · Alignment scoring.

Real evaluation

Each policy receives 10 trials per task with pre-collected initial layouts replayed for consistency. Three independent evaluators score recorded trials under a double-blind protocol; the trial score is their average. Scores account for full success and intermediate substeps. The real “Insert Tubes” rubric uses 0, 0.2, 0.5, and 1.0 for zero through three fully inserted tubes: its weights differ from the simulation rubric. Normalize units before comparing score scales, and do not assume identical scoring merely because names overlap. Paper, real evaluation · Real tube rubric.

RoboDojo-RealEval standardizes hardware, workspace, lighting, resets, deployment interface, and scoring evidence. The paper describes public evaluation layouts plus hidden verification layouts to check submission consistency. This improves reproducibility, but it does not turn real and simulated tasks into a direct sim-to-real transfer experiment.

Seven concrete ways to design a different benchmark

The following are proposals derived from the comparison, not claims that every existing benchmark omits every feature.

1. Matched real / sim industrial procedures

Extend DataCenterBench-style tasks to the same real components and digital scenes, following SIMPLER’s validation logic. Measure ranking agreement separately from absolute completion. Vary insertion force, connector latch, cable stiffness, and access clearance independently. This would test whether simulation is useful for selecting a deployment policy.

2. Full procedures with functional outcomes

Chain identification, isolation, physical intervention, verification, and restoration. Score each required stage, the final operating condition, and invalid ordering. Instrument outcomes where possible: continuity / connectivity, keypress events, latch engagement, torque, or device telemetry. A visually plausible connection should not be sufficient when functionality is the goal.

3. Deliberate recovery tests

Introduce a slipped grasp, shifted target, dropped object, or unexpected jam at a controlled point. Compare recovery success, additional time, interventions, and damage with a clean baseline. This separates robustness from lucky first-attempt execution.

4. Factorial capability diagnosis

For the same physical task, independently vary visual clutter, occlusion duration, tolerance, number of dependent stages, and instruction novelty. Report a performance surface or paired differences. This can reveal whether failure comes from perception, memory, motor precision, or composition rather than attaching several labels to an unrelated task set.

5. Learning under maintenance and drift

Borrow LIBERO’s sequential evaluation, but introduce a new connector type, a camera shift, or altered gripper friction over time. Re-test old tasks after adaptation. Measure sample efficiency, retention, and stability on hardware. This connects lifelong learning to a concrete operational change.

6. Reliability, cost, and intervention together

Report full completion, partial credit, elapsed time, inference cost, resets, operator interventions, and thermal / collision stops as separate outcomes. Include human or teleoperation reference performance. Avoid a single weighted score that lets cheap incomplete behavior appear equivalent to reliable completion.

7. Replication across physical sites

Use multiple rigs, operators, camera placements, and sites; pre-register what is held fixed and what varies. Publish trial-level evidence and failure reasons, include uncertainty at the task and rig level, and reserve private variants for final evaluation. This would expose setup dependence that fixed-rig scores cannot measure.

A particularly useful combination for Robocurve

Use DataCenterBench’s real procedural grounding, RoboDojo’s dimension-level diagnosis, SIMPLER’s matched sim–real validation, and LIBERO’s retention tests. Add functional instrumentation and multi-rig replication. RoboCasa and BEHAVIOR contribute the missing notion of a complete, stateful job spanning several locations and objects. This is a design proposal, not a validated benchmark recipe.

Choose a benchmark by the claim you want to make

“My policy forgets less.”
Use sequential LIBERO or RoboCasa lifelong protocols; report retention curves, not only a final mean.

“My simulator helps choose real policies.”
Use SIMPLER’s matched evaluations and rank-agreement analysis on the relevant setup.

“My robot does useful household work.”
Use BEHAVIOR or RoboCasa composites; distinguish simulator success from physical deployment evidence.

“My agent can operate real controls.”
Use DataCenterBench and add domain-specific functional checks and physical variation.

“My manipulation policy has broad capabilities.”
Use RoboDojo’s dimension breakdown with per-task evidence; keep simulation and real results separate.

“My system is operationally reliable.”
Combine longer procedures, recovery, multiple sites, and intervention / cost accounting. No single scoped benchmark here proves this alone.

11 / Reading any robotics score

Ask what the denominator hides.

  1. Which release and task inventory? Pin code, assets, camera settings, controller, horizon, and reset states. Registry aliases are not necessarily new tasks.
  2. What data was allowed? Separate task-specific fine-tuning from general pretraining, and task novelty from visual novelty. “Unseen” is always relative to a specified data pool.
  3. What is success? Read the goal checker, numeric tolerances, release / return-to-origin rules, and any event-history constraints. An instruction, a task name, and a checker can describe different levels of behavior.
  4. Who observes the outcome? Simulator state is precise within the simulator; cameras can miss functionality; human and model judges introduce interpretation. A stronger evaluator can have privileged information unavailable to the policy—state that explicitly.
  5. How is it aggregated? Episode weighting, equal task weighting, and equal dimension weighting answer different questions. Partial score and full success should appear side by side.
  6. Which failures count? Report external faults, reruns, timeouts, thermal stops, interventions, and discarded trials. Avoid silently changing the tested system with policy-specific harness help.
  7. How uncertain is the result? Record trial counts, seeds, task and rig coverage, and intervals. Small leaderboard differences can reflect sampling or setup rather than better general competence.
